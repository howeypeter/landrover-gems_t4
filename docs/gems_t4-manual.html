Land Rover GEMS V8 · diagnostic & emulated-programming tool

gems_t4 Field Manual

Everything in one place: what the tool does, how to install and use it, how to build the K-line adapter, how to wire it on the car and on the bench, and — plainly — what it can and cannot do to a real GEMS ECU. Every hardware pin here is verified against the ST L9637D datasheet and Land Rover GEMS service data.

P38 4.0/4.6 · Disco 1 V8i Lucas/SAGEM GEMS ISO 9141 / KWP2000 K-line Python · Pico + L9637D v0.0.7

01Overview

gems_t4 is a software recreation of the Rover / Land Rover TestBook T4 dealer diagnostic tool, aimed at the Lucas/SAGEM GEMS V8 engine ECU (P38 Range Rover 4.0/4.6 and Discovery 1 V8i, roughly 1995 to early 1999). It ships with a built-in virtual ECU, so the entire program runs on a Windows laptop with no car and no adapter — useful for learning the workflow and for development. When you build the hardware, the same code drives a real ECU over a USB K-line adapter (or over a network link).

Two front-ends share one engine: a Rich command-line interface and a PySide6 "Win98 kiosk" GUI styled after the period tool. As of v0.0.7 it has 12 GUI screens, 40 live-data parameters, and 169 + 235 automated tests (all passing).

i

What this manual assumes

A Windows laptop, a GEMS-era vehicle (or a spare GEMS ECU), and basic comfort with a breadboard and a multimeter. No prior knowledge of the tool is assumed.

i

Not an electrical engineer? Two aids for the jargon

Technical terms with a dotted underline show a plain-English definition when you hover or tap them. And every term is defined in the full Glossary at the end (§17). So you never have to already know what "Vs" or "decoupling" means.

02Safety first

Read these before any 12 V touches the hardware. They are expanded in the wiring sections.

⚠

OBD pin 16 / the +12 V feed is battery-live at all times

It carries +12 V even with the key off. Put an inline 1–2 A fuse on it and never short it to ground.

⚠

Power the transceiver's Vcc from 3.3 V — never 5 V

The L9637D's RX output idles at Vcc. At 5 V it would push 5 V into the Pico's non-5 V-tolerant GPIO and can destroy it. Vcc's operating range is 3–5 V (7 V is the absolute maximum), so 3.3 V from the Pico is well in spec.

⚠

Chip orientation is what actually kills boards

The 12 V must land on pin 7 (Vs) only. A flipped SO-8 or an off-by-one that puts 12 V on pins 1–4 or 8 destroys the chip and possibly the Pico. Find the pin-1 notch and confirm before powering.

i

Start every session read-only, and prove writes on the simulator first

Reads (fault codes, live data) are low-risk. Any write (coding, immobiliser) should be rehearsed against the built-in virtual ECU before you attempt it on real hardware.

03The vehicle: GEMS in brief

GEMS ("Generic Engine Management System", Lucas/SAGEM) ran the Rover V8 4.0/4.6 from the P38's launch until the 1999 model year, when Bosch Motronic M5.2.1 (the "Thor" engine) replaced it. A single ECM handles fuel and ignition — hot-wire MAF metering, distributorless wasted-spark coil packs. "GEMS or Thor?" is the first fork in every P38 engine procedure, so identify the vehicle first.

No CAN bus — one shared K-line

A late-90s Land Rover has no CAN bus. Every diagnosable module hangs off a single shared wire — the ISO 9141 "K-line" on pin 7 of the J1962 OBD socket — at about 10.4 kbit/s, half-duplex, tester-initiated. The tester addresses each module in turn over that one wire; the modules do not talk to each other over it. (CAN only arrives with the BMW-era L322 in 2002.) This is why the real T4 was slow, and why the emulator's "communicating with ECU… please wait" pauses are authentic.

The BeCM and the immobiliser

The BeCM (Body electrical Control Module, under the driver's seat) runs nearly all body electrics and the immobiliser. The GEMS ECM will not run the engine until it receives a coded mobilisation signal from the BeCM. No code, no start — the canon "ENGINE IMMOBILISED" no-start. This matters hugely for the ECU-swap job (§13): re-pairing a different ECU to the car's BeCM is the "Security-Learn" operation, and it needs both modules present.

i

OBD-II is only half-true here

NAS (North-American-spec) petrol V8s are genuinely OBD-II compliant for emissions data. Everything else on the truck — EAS, BeCM, ABS, HEVAC, SRS — speaks proprietary Rover/Lucas protocols on the same K-line. That's exactly why a TestBook was needed and why a generic ELM327 sees almost nothing beyond the engine.

04What it can & can't do — read this

The word "program" means three different things on a GEMS, with very different answers. Getting this straight avoids chasing something that physically cannot exist.

"Programming" means…Over the K-line?Reality
1. The tune — fuel/ignition mapsNo — neverGEMS maps live on write-once EPROM chips (27C512 fuel, 27C1001 ignition). There is no flash and no bootloader in the ECU — nothing can receive a reflash over the wire, on any GEMS variant. Changing maps is a bench chip-swap.
2. Coding / config — VIN last-6, dealer ID, 4.0/4.6 select, transmission, build codeYesSmall settings written over the K-line ($3B). The tool does this, gated (backup → verify → confirm).
3. Immobiliser Security-Learn — re-sync BeCM ↔ ECMYesThe one genuine over-the-wire write. Needs the BeCM present and powered (so: in the car), and — honestly — is unvalidated on real hardware (§14).
⚠

You cannot "remap" a GEMS over OBD — full stop

This was verified against the ST EPROM datasheets and Land Rover / tuning sources. GEMS maps live on write-once EPROM; the only electrically-erasable path is an aftermarket flash-EPROM retrofit (still a bench chip swap). No factory GEMS, and no tool (TestBook, Nanocom, Faultmate), reflashes the GEMS calibration through the diagnostic port. Later Td5 "NNN" diesels and the Thor V8 can be flashed over OBD — but those are different, newer ECUs, not GEMS.

05Install & run

Windows laptop, Python 3.11+ (developed on 3.14). Create a virtual environment and install:

python -m venv .venvcreate the venv
.venv\Scripts\Activate.ps1activate (PowerShell)
pip install -e ".[gui]"install with the GUI; or pip install -r requirements.txt for CLI-only
python -m gems_t4 scenariossmoke test — lists the four fault scenarios, no hardware needed

Run the GUI: python -m gems_t4 gui, or double-click launch_gui.bat. The launcher runs the live venv source (not a pre-built exe), so it always reflects the current code — nothing to "update" beyond a git pull. Add --instant to skip the period-authentic "communicating with ECU" pauses during testing. The version (gems_t4 v0.0.7) shows on the boot splash and window title.

PySide6 (the GUI) is intentionally kept out of requirements.txt — it's a large optional dependency, installed via the [gui] extra.

06Using the tool

The flow is guided, T4-style: identify the vehicle, pick a function. Everything below works against the virtual ECU with no hardware, and against a real ECU once connected.

FunctionWhat it does
Vehicle IDVIN-first identification (the "GEMS or Thor?" fork); on the simulator it also picks the fault scenario.
Fault codesRead and clear stored DTCs (P0118, P0303, P1185, …). Clearing wipes all codes — there is no per-code clear on GEMS — and asks for confirmation.
Live data40 $61 measures on analog gauges (temps, RPM, MAF, throttle, O2, fuel trims, injector pulse width, coil charge, per-cylinder misfire counts, …). Watching more at once slows the refresh — that's the K-line, modelled honestly.
Actuator testsMIL, O2 heater, fuel pump relay, A/C, condenser fan — with the safety refusals (e.g. the fuel pump won't run with the engine running: "test not available while the engine is running").

i

Network links are read-only by default

Over a network transport, write services (coding, actuators, Security-Learn) are refused unless you explicitly pass --allow-writes on a link you trust. Reads and DTC-clear stay allowed. This gate is enforced on the laptop side; keep serve on localhost or behind an SSH tunnel for real use.

i

This is chip work, not part of the K-line adapter

To edit the fuel/ignition maps you'd need an EPROM programmer (e.g. TL866 / Willem) and replacement EPROMs — or windowless flash-EPROM equivalents (e.g. an SST27SF512 for the 27C512, plus a 1 Mbit flash-EPROM equivalent for the 27C1001) so you can re-burn without a UV eraser. The chip comes out of the ECU and goes in the programmer; none of this happens over the diagnostic port.

09Building the adapter

The Pico owns all K-line timing (5-baud init, byte windows, half-duplex echo cancellation); the L9637D just level-shifts between the Pico's 3.3 V UART and the car's 12 V K-line. Flash the firmware onto the Pico (Arduino-Pico core; see the project's firmware/ folder), then wire it up.

The two extra parts, exactly where they go

  • 510 Ω pull-up — pin 6 (K) to pin 7 (Vs). One leg on each pin; they connect through the 510 Ω (not a bare wire). The K-line is open-drain — devices only pull it low — so this resistor is what holds it high. Required.
  • 100 nF cap — pin 3 (Vcc) to GND (pin 5), close to the chip. Steadies the logic supply. Recommended; no polarity.
✓

Read and write use the same wiring

The K-line is a single bidirectional wire and the L9637D's TX/RX handle both directions, so reading and writing use the identical adapter. You never re-wire to switch between read-only and write mode — that's a software setting, not a hardware one.

Powering the adapter from the Pico — use pin 36

The L9637D's VCC (pin 3) is fed 3.3 V from the Pico. On a Raspberry Pi Pico / Pico 2 / Pico 2 W the 3.3 V comes from physical pin 36, labelled 3V3(OUT) — the regulated output of the Pico's onboard regulator. It's in the bottom-right corner (USB port at top). Several nearby pins look similar; only pin 36 is correct:

PinLabelUse for VCC?
363V3(OUT)Yes — 3.3 V regulated output → L9637D VCC (pin 3). Sources ~300 mA, ample here.
373V3_ENNo — regulator enable (already tied high). Pulling it low switches the Pico's 3.3 V off.
39VSYSNo — ~5 V system input, not 3.3 V.
40VBUSNo — raw 5 V from USB.
35ADC_VREFNo — analog reference, not a power rail.

For ground, any of the Pico's eight GND pins works; pin 38 sits right next to pin 36 and is the tidy choice → L9637D GND (pin 5). Pin 36 is an output — power flows out of it to the transceiver; never feed 5 V or 12 V back into it.

10Wiring: on-car (via OBD-II)

For diagnostics on the car — and for the immobiliser work — you plug into the OBD-II (J1962) socket. The transceiver bridges the Pico's 3.3 V logic to the car's single 12 V K-line, powered from the car's battery feed at the socket.

11Wiring: bench rig (no car)

To test the adapter against a spare ECU with no vehicle, power both the ECU and the adapter from a PC PSU and wire the adapter straight to the ECU's connectors. On an ATX supply, yellow = +12 V, black = ground, and you jumper the green wire to a black to switch it on — a small toggle switch there makes a tidy master on/off (see the switch list in §08). A second toggle on the ignition line (ECU pin 8) lets you cycle "ignition" without dropping main power. Everything shares that one ground.

⚠

Wire colours & which way round the connector is

Two-colour wires. Land Rover uses the Lucas code — a base colour plus a thinner tracer stripe, written base-then-tracer. So LG/R (or LGR) is a light-green wire with a red stripe, and B/P is black with a purple stripe. Letter key: N brown · U blue · G green · LG light-green · W white · R red · P purple · S slate/grey · B black · K pink · O orange · Y yellow.

The pins are mirrored between the harness and the ECU. A connector's pin layout is left-right reversed depending on which face you look at: the view of the ECU's pin/socket face is the mirror image of the view of the mating harness plug's wire-entry face. What sits on the left as you look at one half sits on the right on the other. Always read the pin number moulded beside each terminal and count from there — never assume left-to-right runs the same way on both halves.

Do not trust colour for the +12 V pins. Wire colours vary by year and market, and a wrong guess on pin 7 or 8 can destroy the ECU. Confirm the two power pins by pin number and a multimeter (they read battery voltage) before you apply 12 V; use colour only as a cross-check. Authoritative per-pin colours are in the RAVE wiring diagram for your exact VIN.

⚠

Meter-check the ECU pins before the yellow wire goes live

These pins are from Land Rover GEMS service data (cross-checked against two independent factory-derived pinouts; the coil/MIL/fuel-pump pins validated exactly). But the source tables are Discovery/Morgan GEMS. On a P38 Range Rover the same three connectors are labelled C505 (36-way black, outputs), C507 (36-way red, K-line) and C509 (18-way black, power/ground) — the power/ground pins on C509 match the C1033 numbers above, but the K-line pin can differ on a P38, so meter-verify it. Since 12 V on the wrong pin kills an ECU, on your ECU: ohm pins 5/9/10/16 to the ECU case (≈0 Ω = grounds), and confirm the K-line pin reads continuous to OBD socket pin 7 (NAS/US ≈ pin 23, UK/Euro ≈ pin 20).

i

Coding works on the bench; the immobiliser does not

With only the ECU (no BeCM), you can read its ID, fault codes and live data, and exercise coding writes (VIN last-6, dealer ID, 4.0/4.6 select, transmission — these are straight ECU writes, no BeCM needed). Only Security-Learn is a BeCM↔ECM handshake, so it alone needs the BeCM present — that's a car job (§13). The transient clamp on Vs is also a pre-car item — a bench PSU's 12 V is clean.

12Bench → car workflow

Prove the tool on the bench, then move to the car. The two wiring diagrams map to the two stages.

Stage 1 — bench (validate the adapter)

  1. Wire the adapter + 510 Ω pull-up; Vcc from 3V3. Triple-check pin-1 orientation.
  2. Wire the bench rig (§11): PSU +12 V → ECU pin 7 & pin 8; grounds common; K-line to C1017.
  3. Meter-check grounds and the K-line pin (callout above). No transient clamp needed at this stage.
  4. Power the PSU (flip the master switch, or join green→black). Flash/confirm the Pico; find its COM port.
  5. First contact is read-only: gems_t4 live --port COMx, read the ECU ID and fault codes. Success here means your hardware and the read path work.

Stage 2 — car (attempt the immobiliser sync)

  1. Fit the ECU in the car. Add the Vs transient clamp and the fuse before live vehicle power.
  2. Connect via the OBD-II socket (§10) — that puts you on the K-line with the ECU and the BeCM.
  3. Ignition on, engine off (an immobilised engine can't run anyway) so both modules are awake.
  4. Do reads first. The one thing that needs the car is the immobiliser Security-Learn (it's the BeCM↔ECM handshake) — attempt it here. (Coding you can already have done on the bench.) Keep the word "attempt" — see §14.

13The ECU-swap job

Common goal: fit a spare ECU to a different car. Whether it works depends mostly on the ECU matching the car — not on software.

1 · Does the spare ECU physically match?

  • Engine size (4.0 vs 4.6): the maps are baked into the chips for a displacement. A 4.6 ECU on a 4.0 (or vice-versa) is calibrated wrong for that engine, and no software fixes it — the maps are on the chips. Same displacement = fine; different = you'd need the correct chips (or the correct ECU).
  • Year / market / spec: big differences (NAS vs non-NAS, very different model year) can bring other mismatches. Closer is better.

2 · The immobiliser pairing — the "will it start" step

A GEMS ECU is locked to its own car's BeCM. Drop a different ECU in and the new car's BeCM won't recognise it → "ENGINE IMMOBILISED." Re-pairing them is Security-Learn, done in the car with the BeCM present. This is the operation the tool implements — but read §14 before relying on it.

3 · Coding to match

Some config lives in the ECU (VIN last-6, dealer, 4.0/4.6 select, transmission) and is writable over the K-line — the tool does this. Note that much VIN / market / EKA coding lives in the BeCM, not the engine ECU.

14Limitations & honest status

⚠

Unproven on real hardware

Everything works against the built-in simulator. The tool has never talked to a physical GEMS ECU — on-car validation (the Pico adapter path) is the pending step this manual helps you reach. Treat every real-hardware result as an experiment until you've confirmed it.

i

The protocol is a best-effort recreation

The exact GEMS wire bytes — especially the immobiliser handshake — are not publicly documented. The tool's protocol is stylized (drawn from Andrew Revill's MEMS3 reverse-engineering plus inference), so a real GEMS ECU may not respond exactly as modelled. This is the biggest uncertainty for the immobiliser write specifically.

i

It is not a replacement for a Nanocom / Faultmate

This is a hobby / learning project, not a commercial tool. For reads and diagnostics it can become genuinely useful once validated. For the immobiliser ECU↔BeCM sync — the one operation where "it's an experiment" isn't good enough if you need the car to start — the known-reliable tools are a Nanocom or Faultmate (whose makers reverse-engineered these protocols). A sensible plan: use this tool to learn and to prove your hardware/diagnostics, and budget for a Nanocom for the immobiliser sync.

Also not built (yet)

Engine-ECU only — the other P38 modules (EAS air suspension, ABS, BeCM, airbags, climate) aren't covered yet. No service adjustments (timing/idle) yet. No guided fault-tree wizards yet.

15Technical reference

Adapter circuit (schematic)

EE-grade circuit diagram of the adapter — standard symbols, component designators, and net labels. K-line only (the L9637D's L-line is unused: LI ties to GND, LO is no-connect). Read and write share this identical circuit; the read-only/write gate lives in software (KwpClient), not in the hardware. Values and pin functions are drawn from the ST L9637 datasheet (Doc ID 1765 Rev 7).

Connector & chip pinouts

The transceiver's own pinout, and the two GEMS ECM connectors you land on for a bench rig. Pin functions are from Land Rover GEMS service data; physical position is not fabricated here — locate each pin by the number moulded on the connector and confirm it with a multimeter before applying 12 V.

Architecture

Four strict layers: transport/ (the only I/O — virtual ECU, Pico adapter, TCP, FTDI stub), protocol/ (KWP2000/ISO-14230 framing, init, services, the KwpClient), gems/ (GEMS meaning — DTCs, live data, actuators, immobiliser, maps, virtual ECU), app/ (Rich CLI + PySide6 GUI). The load-bearing seam is KwpClient(transport) — it takes a real serial transport or the in-memory virtual one, so the whole stack is testable off-car.

Wire protocol (stylized)

Frame: [0x80][target][source][len][data…][checksum], checksum = 8-bit sum mod 256. Services used: $10 session, $27 security, $21→$61 read-data, $18 read DTCs, $14 clear, $30 actuator, $31 routines (immobiliser), $3B write-coding, $1A read-ID, $3E tester-present. Unsupported live-data IDs answer $7F. There are deliberately no $34/$36/$37 download/transfer services — GEMS has no reflash path. The Pico↔host link is a separate length-prefixed USB protocol (0xA5/0x5A frames, crc8) — the Pico is a timed byte pipe, all KWP/GEMS logic lives in Python.

16Backlog / roadmap

What isn't done yet, in plain terms — so you know where the project stands. None of these are features today.

The big one — prove it on real hardware

Everything works against the built-in virtual (simulated) ECU, but the tool has never talked to a real GEMS ECU. On-bench and on-car validation with the Pico adapter is the top item — it's what turns this from a simulator into a real tool. Until then, treat reads as unproven and every write as an experiment.

Features not built yet

  • The rest of the truck. Engine ECU only today. Planned: the other P38 modules — air suspension (EAS), ABS, the body computer (BeCM), airbags, climate control.
  • Service adjustments — the ignition-timing and idle nudges the real T4 could do.
  • An injector output test.
  • Step-by-step guided fault-finding — today you pick from menus; the plan is guided walk-throughs.
  • A wireless (WiFi) adapter option — the laptop side is built; the Pico WiFi firmware is not.
  • Message-centre text and a true full-screen kiosk mode — period-fidelity polish.

Open research (not started)

  • Does EPROM programmability vary by model year? The stance is "write-once chip, bench swap only" — we want to confirm that holds for every GEMS variant.
  • The 4.0/4.6 setting as a real feature — it matters when an ECU ends up on the wrong-size engine.
  • Matching a used / parts-car ECU to a different engine — spelling out exactly what's fixable by coding + immobiliser re-sync vs. what forces a chip swap or a different ECU (see §13).

Known rough edges

  • Write safety-gates are partial. Coding writes enforce backup + verify + confirm (three of the seven gates once advertised); security-access, precondition and dry-run gates aren't built.
  • The "network is read-only" rule is enforced on the laptop, not the serve bridge — fine on localhost, but it wants server-side enforcement before any real over-the-network use.
  • "Immobilised" isn't fully coherent in the simulator — the engine still shows RPM when set to the immobilised state.

Recently done (v0.0.6–v0.0.7): the persistent connection indicator + link test, the "Clear ALL codes" clarity, the fuel-pump refusal reason, live-data 37→40 parameters, and this hardware wiring reference.

17Glossary

Plain-English definitions for the technical terms in this manual. Terms with a dotted underline elsewhere show these same definitions on hover.

ECU / ECM
The engine's computer. On a GEMS car, one unit runs both fuel and ignition.
BeCM
Body electrical Control Module — the P38's big body computer under the driver's seat. It runs the immobiliser, among much else.
Immobiliser
Anti-theft: the ECU won't let the engine run unless the BeCM sends it a secret coded "OK" at each start.
Security-Learn
The procedure that re-pairs the ECU and BeCM so they share that secret again. The one genuine over-the-wire write on a GEMS — and it needs the BeCM present (a car job, not a bench job).
K-line
The single diagnostic wire the tool talks over. It idles "high" (~12 V); devices briefly pull it down to 0 V to send data.
OBD-II / J1962
The 16-pin diagnostic socket under the dash. Pin 7 = K-line, pin 16 = +12 V (always live), pins 4/5 = ground.
ISO 9141 / KWP2000
The rules — the "language" — spoken on the K-line.
Transceiver (L9637D)
The chip that bridges the car's 12 V K-line to the Pico's 3.3 V logic. It only level-shifts; the Pico does all the thinking.
Vs (supply voltage)
The "V" is voltage, the "s" is supply. On the L9637D chip it's the battery-side supply pin (pin 7), fed by the car/battery +12 V.
Vcc (supply voltage)
The "V" is voltage; "cc" is an old convention for a chip's positive supply rail (originally "collector-to-collector" in transistor circuits). On the L9637D it's the low-voltage logic-supply pin (pin 3) — feed it 3.3 V from the Pico, never 5 V.
GND / ground / 0 V
The common reference every part shares. On a PC power supply it's the black wire.
Pull-up (resistor)
A resistor that gently holds a wire "high" when nothing is pulling it low. The K-line needs one (510 Ω, from K to Vs).
Open-drain
A wire that devices can only pull down to 0 V; it relies on a pull-up to ever read "high." That's how the K-line works.
GPIO
A general-purpose input/output pin on the Pico. The Pico's are not "5 V-tolerant" — 5 V on one can damage it.
SO-8
A tiny 8-pin surface-mount chip package (how the L9637D ships). It needs a breakout board to use on a breadboard.
DIP-8 / breakout board
DIP-8 is the older through-hole package that plugs straight into a breadboard; a "breakout" board adapts a surface-mount (SO-8) chip to DIP pins.
Capacitor
A component that stores a little electrical charge; used here to steady voltages and filter noise.
Decoupling capacitor
A small capacitor placed across a chip's power pins to smooth out electrical noise. Recommended on Vcc.
nF / µF (nanofarad / microfarad)
Capacitor sizes. 100 nF = 0.1 µF (often marked "104" on the part).
Ω (ohm)
The unit of electrical resistance. The K-line pull-up is 510 Ω.
mA (milliamp)
A unit of electrical current — one-thousandth of an amp. The ignition-sense line draws only a few mA.
TVS / transient suppressor
A part that clamps (caps) brief high-voltage spikes to protect the chip.
Standoff voltage
The voltage a TVS ignores; above it, the TVS starts clamping. Choose one that clamps below the chip's 40 V limit.
Load-dump / transient
A brief voltage spike on a running car's 12 V (e.g. when the alternator load suddenly changes). A clean bench PSU doesn't produce these.
Fuse
A sacrificial link that blows on a short or overcurrent, protecting the wiring. Put a 1–2 A one on the +12 V feed.
ATX / PC PSU
A computer power supply, used here as a bench 12 V source: yellow = +12 V, black = ground.
PS_ON
The ATX supply's green wire. Join it to a black (ground) wire to switch the supply on.
SPST switch
Single-Pole, Single-Throw — a plain on/off toggle switch.
EPROM
The write-once memory chip that holds the GEMS fuel/ignition maps. Changed by physically swapping the chip — not over the wire.
EPROM programmer
A bench device (e.g. a TL866) that reads and writes those chips once they're out of the ECU.
Multimeter
A meter for voltage / resistance / continuity — essential for the pre-power checks.
Continuity
Whether two points are electrically joined (reads ≈0 Ω on a meter).
DTC
Diagnostic Trouble Code — a fault code (e.g. P0118).
Live data
Real-time sensor readings streamed from the ECU.
Actuator test
Commanding an output (fuel pump, warning lamp, etc.) on or off to check it works.
Coding
Small writable settings in the ECU — VIN last-6, dealer ID, 4.0/4.6 select, transmission. Writable over the wire.
UART
The simple serial format the Pico uses to talk to the transceiver.
Half-duplex
One wire, one direction at a time — how the K-line works.
NAS / non-NAS
North American Spec vs rest-of-world. On a GEMS ECU it changes which pin is the K-line (NAS = pin 23, non-NAS = pin 20).
Virtual ECU
The built-in simulated ECU, so the whole tool runs with no car and no hardware.
Firmware
The small program that runs on the Pico itself.
